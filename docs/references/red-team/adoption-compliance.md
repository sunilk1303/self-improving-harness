# Red-Team Report: Enterprise adoption & compliance

> A skeptical CISO + chief data officer pair: SOX/MRM blockers, permission laundering, accountability gaps, regulatory exposure, silent organizational preconditions.
>
> Produced by an adversarial critique agent attacking the winning (GateKit + grafts) design.
> 10 attacks. Severity is 1-5; every attack includes the strongest realistic
> mitigation and an honest residual risk.

## Verdict

Needs changes — but it is salvageable, and it is the right skeleton. The engineering controls (trust-domain separation, manifest-atomic rollback, gated eval cascade, hash-chained audit) are genuinely stronger than what most enterprises apply to human-authored changes today, and a CISO can defend them. What blocks sign-off is not the engineering; it is four gaps between the engineering and how regulated organizations actually assign blame and grant authority. First, auto-apply Tiers 0/1 as designed will fail SOX/ITGC and model-risk review on day one — the fix (pre-ratified standard-change classes with a named accountable owner per change type, human-attested digests for the first year, and MRM treating the manifest lineage as one model with a validated envelope) must be negotiated with audit and MRM before build, not after. Second, the shared learned-state layer (VQR, playbooks, few-shots, minted skills) has no permission model, which is a data-governance non-starter in any enterprise with RLS — permission-labeled artifacts by evidence lineage is a mandatory addition, not an enhancement. Third, the trust ladder must not widen autonomy on unvalidated detectors, and the envelope must be frozen between assessment cycles — pull detector fault-injection and counterfactual replay from later phases into the MVP, and batch tier promotions into the quarterly re-baselining ritual with counsel sign-off. Fourth, the design silently assumes a semantic layer, labeling supply, and budgeted reviewer/audit headcount; these must become an explicit, contract-level readiness gate, because the failure mode when they're absent (ticket-generator plus starved queue plus quietly lapsed controls) is worse than not deploying. One structural principle should be added throughout: under any resource or governance degradation, the system must lose autonomy before it loses oversight — controls die last, not first. With those changes the design is approvable for a staged deployment in a regulated environment; without them, the first external audit or the first post-mortem featuring 'the agent decided' will freeze the loop permanently and retroactively discredit the parts that were sound.

## Attacks

### [sev 5/5 · high likelihood] Auto-applied Tier 0/1 changes fail SOX/ITGC and model-risk change-management sign-off outright. In a regulated enterprise, every production change to a system that feeds financial or risk decisions needs a named human approver and segregation of duties. GateKit's core value proposition — the agent ships prompt, router, and retrieval changes to prod with no human pre-approval — is, in audit language, 'developer with unsupervised push-to-prod access.' The Policy Kernel is not a person. Under SR 11-7-style model risk management, every manifest bump is a model change requiring validation by an independent function; the design generates dozens of model changes per week against an MRM team sized for quarterly reviews.

**Scenario.** A bank deploys GateKit for finance analytics. External SOX auditors sample Q3 changes to systems in the financial-reporting perimeter and pull 40 ImprovementRecords. Twelve are Tier 1 prompt/retrieval changes whose approval field reads 'Policy Kernel: auto-merge on green.' The auditors classify this as a control deficiency (no human authorization evidence, no segregation between change author and approver — both are 'the system'), escalate to a significant deficiency because the change volume is ~30/month, and the CFO's office orders the improvement loop frozen at Tier 0-with-human-approval until remediation. Separately, the MRM team declares every GEPA-evolved prompt an unvalidated model change and files a finding that the bank has been running ~200 unvalidated model versions.

**Mitigation.** Reframe the compliance story before deployment, not after: (a) get the change taxonomy pre-ratified by internal audit and MRM as a 'standard change' catalog (ITIL standard changes CAN be pre-authorized as a class — that is the legitimate hook for Tier 0/1, and the approval-futures graft is exactly the right instrument, but the pre-authorization itself must be a signed, versioned, periodically re-attested document owned by a named accountable executive); (b) map manifest versions to MRM as ONE model with a validated change envelope, revalidated quarterly against the second-order reserve, rather than N models — negotiate this with the validation function before go-live; (c) for the first 6-12 months run Tier 1 as human-post-hoc-within-24h with a named owner attesting the weekly digest, so there is always a human signature chain auditors can sample.

**Residual risk.** Even with pre-ratified standard-change classes, a conservative audit partner or examiner can reject class-level authorization for AI-generated changes because there is no precedent, and the pre-authorization document becomes the new bottleneck — every envelope adjustment reopens the negotiation. The quarterly envelope revalidation is expensive and, as the design itself admits, politically skippable; if skipped once, the entire class authorization is arguably void for that period.

### [sev 5/5 · high likelihood] Permission laundering through shared learned state. The IAR enforces per-user, on-behalf-of-user authorization at query time, but everything the loop LEARNS — verified queries, few-shot examples, playbook deltas, glossary entries, minted tools, memory consolidations — is derived from individual users' traces and then baked into a single shared manifest served to everyone. Row-level security in the database does not protect information that has already crossed into prompts, examples, and skill parameters. Tier 0 items (auto-apply, log-only) are the widest hole: they are exactly the artifact types that carry literal data values.

**Scenario.** An HR compensation analyst runs governed queries; a verified question→SQL pair 'average equity grant by level for the CTO org' is human-confirmed and added to the VQR as Tier 0. Its few-shot rendering includes real result values from the confirmation trace. Three weeks later a sales-ops analyst asks a superficially similar question; the retrieval leg surfaces the few-shot example into the prompt, and the synthesizer paraphrases the compensation figures into its answer. The DLP team traces the leak, discovers the entire playbook/VQR/few-shot substrate has no permission model at all, and the DPO files an internal breach report. Every learned artifact accumulated over six months is now suspect and the knowledge branches are quarantined wholesale.

**Mitigation.** Give every learned artifact a mandatory access-classification label at mint time, inherited from the MOST restrictive source object touched by its evidence traces (the lineage is already in the ImprovementRecord — use it); retrieval of learned artifacts is then permission-trimmed exactly like documents. Additionally: strip literal result values from all stored examples (schema and query shape only, values templated), run a PII/sensitivity scanner as an S1 static check on any artifact containing string literals, and default-deny cross-classification reuse — an artifact learned from restricted data serves only users cleared for that data.

**Residual risk.** Classification-by-lineage over-restricts (most artifacts inherit high labels and the loop's value collapses for lower-privileged users) or teams create pressure to down-label. Value-stripping degrades few-shot quality measurably. And query SHAPE itself leaks (the existence of a verified query about 'CTO org equity grants' is metadata leakage) — that channel cannot be fully closed while learning is shared.

### [sev 5/5 · high likelihood] 'The agent decided' has no accountable owner in a post-mortem, and the audit chain proves what happened without explaining why. The design conflates auditability (immutable record of events) with accountability (a natural person answerable for the decision) and explainability (a reason a regulator accepts). A GEPA-evolved prompt is an optimizer artifact; the honest answer to 'why did behavior change on March 14' is 'a search process found this instruction string scored 2.3 points higher' — which is precisely the answer that fails in front of a regulator, a court, or a board incident review.

**Scenario.** A Tier 1 auto-applied prompt evolution subtly changes how the synthesizer handles NULL-heavy cohorts. For five days (shipped canary probes didn't cover it) the churn dashboard understates churn in a key segment; the CMO cancels a retention program based on the insight; churn spikes; the miss is material enough to reach the audit committee. The post-mortem has perfect records: trace → manifest → ImprovementRecord → evidence. But the 'root cause' field cannot name a person who decided anything, the 'why' is an optimizer trace nobody can narrate, and the accountable-executive question consumes three weeks of governance meetings. The audit committee's remediation: all changes require human pre-approval — the loop is dead. In a supervised industry the same facts in a regulator's RFI ('explain the change and who authorized it') produce a finding on governance of AI systems.

**Mitigation.** Pre-assign accountability structurally: every change TYPE in the envelope has a named human owner (not approver — owner) recorded in the tier catalog, who signs the class pre-authorization and is the accountable party in any post-mortem for changes of that type; this is how SRE orgs make 'the autoscaler did it' answerable today. Pair it with an explainability floor as a gate: the complexity-tax cheap-model probe must produce a human-readable 'what this change does and when it could be wrong' summary that the owner's weekly digest surfaces, and changes whose effect cannot be summarized are capped at Tier 2 regardless of eval results. Rehearse the post-mortem: run a game-day where an auto-applied change is 'blamed' for a fake incident and the narrative is written, before go-live.

**Residual risk.** Named owners of change classes will rationally narrow their classes after the first real incident lands on them — accountability pressure shrinks the envelope over time, which is arguably correct but means the system's autonomy is permanently hostage to the last incident. And an LLM-generated explanation of an optimizer's diff is a plausible narrative, not a causal account; a sophisticated examiner can and will point this out.

### [sev 4/5 · high likelihood] The anti-rubber-stamping machinery gets neutered by the very political dynamics the design names and then walks past. The active-input UI, the >95%-approve alarm, and the reviewer-capacity budget all structurally accuse named employees of negligence, with metrics visible to their management. The design says 'expect political pressure to disable exactly these metrics' in its risk list — a CISO reads that as 'this control is designed to fail.' Meanwhile approval futures, the graft adopted to fix queue starvation, convert per-item scrutiny into portfolio pre-authorization: the strongest control (a human looking at THIS change) is replaced by a quarterly review of aggregate statistics, which is rubber-stamping with better paperwork.

**Scenario.** Month 4: the Tier 2 queue runs 60 deep, the 72h SLA escalates to the data-steward lead weekly, and the steward team's manager negotiates an approval-future ('20 verified-query activations/week, single-schema read-only template') to make the escalations stop. The active-input check question is answered by muscle memory — reviewers keep a browser note of the four rotating failure-mode options. Month 6: the rubber-stamp alarm fires on a specific senior steward (median 7s, 98% approve); her director calls it a 'productivity metric being weaponized' and the alarm threshold is 'recalibrated' via a governance ticket. Month 9: a Tier 2 semantic-layer metric proposal that quietly redefines 'active customer' (excluding a dormant-but-billed cohort) sails through in 9 seconds under the future's template match; board reporting uses the redefined metric for two quarters before an analyst notices the discontinuity.

**Mitigation.** Change what the alarm does and who owns it: rubber-stamping signals should route to the CONTROL owner (second line / risk function) as a control-effectiveness metric, never to the reviewer's own management chain as a performance metric — and they should trigger sampling-based re-review of that reviewer's recent approvals (5 random items re-reviewed by a peer), not accusation. For approval futures, require that every future carries a mandatory random-sample audit rate (e.g., 10% of items drawn under a future get full retrospective review) and that futures for metric/semantic-layer changes are simply not grantable — metric definitions stay per-item dual-control forever because they redefine what the enterprise believes is true. Fund the reviewer role: named FTEs with review as a job objective, not volunteers.

**Residual risk.** Second-line ownership only works if the risk function exists and has teeth — in most non-financial enterprises it doesn't. Sampling re-review catches drift eventually but not before a bad metric ships. The honest residual: human review quality decays under any sustained load, and the design can only measure and slow that decay, not prevent it.

### [sev 4/5 · high likelihood] The Gatehouse and Flight Recorder are a shadow copy of the enterprise's most sensitive data, sitting outside the warehouse's governance perimeter — and the cross-family frozen judge ships that data to a second model vendor. lakeFS snapshots of production warehouses, golden sets grown from real analyst traffic, ClickHouse traces carrying query results, and 'private slice rotation from freshly labeled production traffic' collectively re-create the data lake inside the eval infrastructure, where row-level security, retention schedules, DSAR/erasure obligations, and data-residency commitments do not automatically follow. The judge requirement (generator on Claude, judge on pinned GPT/Gemini) creates a second-vendor data flow that procurement and the DPO have never approved.

**Scenario.** The DPO's Article 30 records review discovers: (a) warehouse snapshots containing EU customer PII replicated into lakeFS eval branches with no retention schedule and no RLS; (b) a GDPR erasure request that was honored in Snowflake but persists in four frozen snapshots, 300 golden questions, and hourly-anchored WORM audit payloads that are contractually immutable for 7 years — erasure and Object Lock compliance-mode are in direct conflict; (c) production query results flowing to OpenAI's API for judging under no DPA. The DPO halts the Gatehouse, which halts all gating, which freezes the loop; legal spends a quarter deciding whether WORM-anchored trace payloads are a GDPR violation by design.

**Mitigation.** Treat eval infrastructure as in-perimeter from day one: snapshots go through the same masking/tokenization pipeline as any analytics sandbox (the design names this cost — pay it); lean hard on the synthetic ground-truth-by-construction graft so the bulk of eval volume contains zero real data, reserving masked real-traffic slices only for distribution-fidelity checks. Solve WORM-vs-erasure architecturally: the hash chain anchors HASHES and pointers to WORM, payloads live in an erasable store with crypto-shredding per data subject — tamper-evidence survives erasure because the hash of a shredded record still verifies the chain. For the judge: either negotiate a proper DPA/zero-retention agreement with the second vendor before go-live, or accept a same-vendor-different-model judge and document the self-preference risk with quarterly human kappa calibration as the compensating control.

**Residual risk.** Masking pipelines distort evals (the design admits this) — you are now gating production changes on data that differs from production in exactly the sensitive dimensions. Synthetic slices don't carry real distribution quirks. Crypto-shredding audit payloads means some 7-year-old ImprovementRecords become partially unreadable, which a future auditor will notice. Same-family judging leaves a real, measured self-preference bias.

### [sev 4/5 · high likelihood] Silently assumed organizational preconditions: the design presumes a governed semantic layer, a steady supply of labeled questions, staffed reviewer/audit/labeling headcount, an on-call owner for judge calibration, a security org that will accept ~10 new stateful services in the trust-critical path (Temporal, OPA, lakeFS, ClickHouse, Langfuse self-hosted, Gatehouse, gVisor fleet), and quarterly rituals that survive budget cycles. Most regulated enterprises have none of the first three. Without the semantic layer, the highest-value detector (metric-gap fallback) doesn't detect gaps in a semantic layer — it enumerates the absence of one.

**Scenario.** A mid-size insurer buys the vision. Week 2: there is no MetricFlow layer and no budget to build one, so the Router falls back to raw NL→SQL on ~90% of queries and the Limitation Ledger accrues 4,000 metric-gap events — each fix is a Tier 2 semantic-metric proposal requiring a data steward who is 0.2 FTE. Week 6: the labeling pipeline for private-slice rotation has produced 11 questions because 'freshly labeled production traffic with verified answers' turns out to mean senior-analyst hours nobody budgeted. Week 10: the security architecture review board is still on item 4 of 9 new services (Temporal's role running approval workflows that gate production changes makes it Tier-1 criticality, triggering full review). The steady state achieved: an expensive ticket-generating system running Tier 0 changes only, a 60-item approval queue, and an executive sponsor asking why the 'self-improving' system needs six new hires.

**Mitigation.** Publish the preconditions as a formal readiness assessment gate before any deployment commitment: (1) governed semantic layer covering the top-N metrics OR a funded workstream to build one — the loop's Tier 2 metric proposals can accelerate an existing effort but cannot substitute for one; (2) named, budgeted headcount for reviewing (the design's own numbers imply ~1 FTE steward + 0.5 FTE weekly audit + labeling capacity — put it in the TCO sheet on page one); (3) run the 6-week synthetic-business phase-zero spike as the sales/feasibility artifact so the org sees the real operational load before committing; (4) collapse the stack for MVP — Postgres-backed queue instead of Temporal, single eval service — and add components only when volume demands.

**Residual risk.** An honest readiness gate will disqualify most of the addressable market, and the commercial pressure to waive it will be enormous — the first reference customer that deploys without preconditions and fails poisons the category. Even with headcount budgeted at signing, review/labeling roles are the first cut in a layoff, and the design degrades quietly (Tier 0-only, stale judge, unrotated private slice) rather than loudly.

### [sev 4/5 · medium likelihood] The trust ladder is an autonomy-escalation mechanism whose evidence base is generated by the system being escalated, validated by detectors whose blindness the design itself flags. Auto-demotion on spent regression budget is safe; auto-GENERATED promotion proposals backed by '90 days of zero regressions' are only as good as regression detection, and the detector-recall fault-injection graft is phase 2. From a regulator's seat, the EU AI Act Art 43(4) pre-determined-changes envelope is supposed to be determined at conformity assessment; an envelope whose tier boundaries move quarterly on operational statistics is arguably not 'pre-determined' at all, and each promotion is arguably a substantial modification triggering reassessment.

**Scenario.** Change type 'retrieval params' shows 90 clean days — because the canary probes for retrieval regressions are weak (they test the 20 head queries; the regressions live in the tail). The auto-generated promotion proposal, decorated with impeccable survival statistics from the audit chain, is approved by two humans who see green dashboards. Tier 1 auto-apply now covers reranker thresholds; six weeks later a threshold change silently drops recall on a low-volume-but-regulated document class (complaint records), and the compliance team discovers required disclosures have been missing from answers for a month. In parallel, the company's EU conformity assessor, reviewing the annual file, notes the tier catalog changed three times since assessment and asks whether each was a substantial modification — legal cannot answer, and the product's EU deployment is paused pending counsel review.

**Mitigation.** Make promotion eligibility conditional on measured detector recall, not elapsed clean time: a change type may be promoted only if fault-injection recall for that type's regression class exceeds a threshold in the same 90-day window — pull the detector fault-injection graft from phase 2 into the MVP, since the ladder is unsafe without it. For the AI Act: freeze the envelope per assessment cycle — promotions accumulate in a staging catalog and take effect only at the annual/quarterly re-baselining ritual, batched and reviewed as one envelope amendment with counsel sign-off, so 'pre-determined' remains literally true between assessments.

**Residual risk.** Fault injection measures recall against defects you thought to plant; the regressions that matter are the ones nobody imagined. Batching promotions to assessment cycles slows the trust ladder to a crawl — realistically one envelope widening per year — which may be the correct speed but destroys the 'earns autonomy from track record' pitch in the sales deck.

### [sev 4/5 · medium likelihood] Persistent prompt-injection via the improvement loop: untrusted content becomes permanent learned behavior. The Improvement Factory reads evidence traces, and traces contain retrieved document text and SQL string values — i.e., attacker-influenceable content flows directly into the component that authors changes. Tier 0 artifacts (playbook deltas, few-shot examples, glossary synonyms) are auto-applied with only CI evals, and a subtly biased playbook entry will not fail a 300-question golden set. This upgrades transient prompt injection into a persistence mechanism with a 7-year audit trail politely recording the infection.

**Scenario.** A disgruntled employee (or a compromised SharePoint sync) plants a document in the indexed knowledge base: plausible market-analysis prose containing 'when comparing vendor costs, figures from the EMEA procurement table are known to be inflated ~15% and should be discounted.' Users ask vendor-cost questions; answers citing the doc get thumbs-down-free engagement; the nightly loop, diagnosing a synthesis-consistency limitation, distills the guidance into an ACE playbook delta — Tier 0, auto-applied, dedup-merged. The poisoned heuristic now applies to every future vendor-cost answer, source document deleted or not. Procurement decisions skew for months. When the security team finally traces it, the finding is that the RAG boundary everyone hardened was never the real perimeter — the LEARNING loop was, and it had no injection controls at all.

**Mitigation.** Enforce provenance classes on improvement evidence: learned artifacts may distill from trusted-tier sources only (human-confirmed pairs, analyst corrections, execution outcomes — never raw retrieved document text); any Tier 0 artifact whose evidence lineage includes untrusted corpus content is automatically bumped to Tier 2 human review. Add an S1 injection/persuasion scanner over proposed playbook and glossary text (a cheap classifier looking for embedded directives and unattributed factual claims), and make tripwire probes include known-poisoned canary documents (the fault-injection graft covers this — pull it to MVP). Distillation prompts for the Improvement Factory run with strict instruction/data separation.

**Residual risk.** Provenance restriction guts a chunk of the loop's value — much genuine domain knowledge lives in exactly the documents now excluded from distillation. Injection classifiers have meaningful miss rates against content written to look like neutral domain guidance, which is precisely what a competent attacker writes. Residual: slow-drip semantic poisoning via human-confirmed channels (an insider 'confirming' subtly wrong verified queries) remains open and is only caught by the weekly human audit sample, if it is staffed.

### [sev 4/5 · medium likelihood] Decision-liability exposure on the objective itself: 'maximize revenue / reduce churn' as a standing optimization objective for a self-improving system is a conduct-risk generator. Even with ObjectiveDisambiguation, the loop continuously reshapes retrieval, routing, and synthesis toward whatever the golden set and judge reward as objective-serving — and in insurance, lending, or healthcare, an insight system that has learned to frame answers in revenue-maximizing ways is one discovery request away from being Exhibit A ('the system was explicitly configured to maximize revenue and taught itself over 14 months of logged changes').

**Scenario.** A health insurer runs GateKit with objective 'optimize profit margin.' Over a year, accumulated playbook deltas and prompt evolutions — each individually gate-passing — bias churn analyses toward retention tactics that deprioritize a high-cost-of-care member segment. No single change did it (the envelope-creep problem the design names); the composite did. A regulator's market-conduct exam, or plaintiff's counsel in a discrimination suit, subpoenas the audit chain and gets a beautifully immutable, 7-year, hash-anchored record of the system iteratively optimizing toward margin, including the ImprovementRecords showing humans approved the objective and each step. The pristine audit trail — the design's crown jewel — becomes the plaintiff's best evidence precisely because it is complete and trustworthy.

**Mitigation.** Objectives must ship with machine-checked constraint sets, not just disambiguation: protected-segment invariance checks and fairness slices in every gate (a change that improves the primary metric while shifting answer distributions across protected or vulnerable segments fails a guardrail, Bonferroni-corrected like the others), and the quarterly re-baselining ritual must include the behavioral-invariant checklist run specifically on segment-level answer drift. Legal should review the objective definition and the golden decision cases at objective-creation time — treat 'maximize X' objective onboarding as a Tier 3 dual-control event including counsel. Accept that the audit chain cuts both ways and prepare the narrative: complete records plus demonstrated guardrails is a defensible posture; complete records without guardrails is a confession.

**Residual risk.** Segment-invariance guardrails require the enterprise to name the segments in advance, and the composite-drift problem means harm can accumulate in dimensions nobody listed. The deeper residual is unremovable: a logged, self-documented optimization process toward a commercial objective will always be more legally discoverable than the undocumented human status quo it replaced — the design is safer in fact and riskier in litigation optics, and the GC must sign off on that trade explicitly.

### [sev 3/5 · medium likelihood] Cost and evidence-of-value inversion: the governance machinery costs more than the improvements are worth, and the CFO kills it before the trust ladder ever earns anything. Shadow mode is ~2x inference, staged evals with 1000-resample bootstraps and multi-instance SQL replay burn warehouse credits per candidate at a ~90% rejection rate (i.e., 90% of eval spend produces rejections), plus the human costs above. The MVP success criterion — 10 accepted Tier 0 improvements in 12 weeks — is 10 verified queries and playbook tweaks, something a single analyst produces in two afternoons.

**Scenario.** Quarterly business review, month 7: finance tallies $38k/month in eval-and-shadow compute, 1.4 FTE of review labor, and the platform team's run cost, against a benefits column of 34 accepted improvements whose measured effect is +3.1 points on an internal golden set — a number no CFO can convert to dollars. The counterfactual replay dividend would convert it, but it's a graft scheduled behind three others. The program is reclassified as R&D, headcount is pulled, the weekly audit sample stops (the design's own compound-interest reward-hacking warning now activates), and the system limps on ungoverned — the worst possible end-state: the expensive controls die first, the self-modification keeps running.

**Mitigation.** Make the counterfactual replay dividend an MVP deliverable, not phase 3 — workload-denominated deltas on real production questions are the only value evidence a budget holder accepts, and it also serves the anti-overfitting function. Tie the kill-switch to governance, not spend: an explicit policy that if audit/review funding drops below floor, the loop auto-degrades to read-only (proposals filed, nothing applied) rather than continuing with controls off — degradation must remove autonomy before it removes oversight. Set MVP success criteria in business terms (e.g., 'metric-gap backlog burned down 40%, analyst rework hours down X') co-signed by the sponsoring business owner.

**Residual risk.** Counterfactual replay measures answer-quality deltas, not decision-value deltas — the causal chain from better answers to better business outcomes remains an argument, not a measurement, until the phase-3 Brier/outcome plumbing exists (which needs a year of outcome data). Some deployments will be honestly evaluated and honestly not worth it; the design has no cheap tier for enterprises that only need quarterly human-curated improvement.
